= Designing a liquid network
:description: Budgeting head, sizing pumps, manifolds, and returning water to a pressurized network.

A liquid network works when every machine's inlet gets the pressure it needs while the network carries the flow they use.
This page shows how to check that before you build.

== The head budget

Start at the source and add up what the fluid spends on its way to the machine:

. *What the source pushes.*
An extractor or a machine's outlet supplies at 10 m of head.
A Fluid Buffer pushes with the height of its liquid surface.
A pump adds its lift, at the flow through it.
. *Minus the climb.*
Every meter up costs a meter of head; every meter down gives one back.
. *Minus friction.*
More for long pipes, high flow, many bends, Mk1 pipe and thick liquids.
. *What has to be left:* the machine's inlet pressure.
Most liquid inlets draw nothing below 4 m and run at full speed from 5 m (see xref:machines.adoc[]).

If the budget doesn't close, add a pump, shorten or straighten the line, change to Mk2 pipe, or lay a second pipe beside the first.

.Example: a Refinery fed straight from an extractor
====
The extractor supplies 10 m.
The Refinery runs at full speed from 5 m.
That leaves 5 m for the climb and the friction: enough for a short, level line, not for one that climbs a cliff.
====

== "Balanced" supply and demand

"Balanced" networks, where supply exactly matches demand, require slightly more careful management, particularly during start-up. Machines refilling their internal buffers will ask for up to twice their rate (see xref:machines.adoc[]). Starting all machines on a network can cause a pressure drop after a surge and take a long time to recover.
See xref:starting-up.adoc#_a_balanced_network[A balanced network]. One way to compensate for various surges is to use a tank on top of a tall structure to form a "water tower".

== "Water towers" and tanks

A liquid tank does not hold pressure (think of it as being open to atmosphere on the top). So a pump upstream of a tank will fill it, but once it's full, not pressurize it. This means that a tank's surface sets the pressure downstream of it.
Pressure upstream of the tank doesn't carry past it.

If you place the tank high relative to the machines requiring the liquid, then you can create a "water tower". Liquid has to be pumped _up_ to the tank, which means the liquid there has a lot of potential head. The tank holds no extra pressure, but the difference in height between the source (the tank) and the sink (the machines that need the liquid) creates the head for the network.

== Manifolds

In FicstFluids a pressurized manifold delivers to every machine on it.
You don't need to pipe one machine straight into the next.

Producers can feed one pipeline and consumers draw from it, as you would build a Plastic or Rubber line.
For example, Refineries making Alumina Solution can feed one manifold that the Aluminum Scrap Refineries draw from.

Watch the head: a Refinery's outlet pushes at 10 m and its inlet runs at full speed from 5 m.
So the producers alone leave about 5 m for the climb and the friction along the manifold (2 m if you want buffers to refill at the fastest rate).
Keep it short and level, or add a pump.

== Returning liquid to the network

A machine's outlet pushes only 10 m of head, plus its height.
Where a well, extractors or a water tower hold the network above that, returned liquid can't get in.
The machine's output backs up and it stops "full" while the network still wants liquid.

*The layout that works:*

. Merge the machines' liquid outlets into one return line.
. Put a pump on the return line, after the merge, so one pump serves them all.
. Join the return to the supply *just after a valve* on the supply.

The valve is one-way, so the pump can't push the returns back up the supply line.
When the returns come in, the pressure past the valve rises and the supply gives less, so the returns go first and the supply only tops up.

*The return pump must beat the supply.*
At the join it has to push above the supply's pressure, plus the friction along the return line.
Otherwise the machines farthest along the return line back up first.
A Mk1 lifts at most 32.7 m, less with flow; if the supply holds the join near that, use two Mk1s in series or a Mk2.

TIP: A flow limit on the supply valve lowers the pressure at the join.
But it is a fixed setting, and starves the consumers if it is set below what they need beyond the returns.

The balance must still close, as in vanilla: if the machines return more water than the network uses, the returns back up whatever the layout.

== Pumps in series or parallel

Pumps in series add pressure; pumps in parallel add flow.
Size each pump for the flow through it, not for the lift alone.
See xref:pumps.adoc#_series_or_parallel[Series or parallel].
