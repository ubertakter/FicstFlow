= Pumps
:description: How much a pump lifts, why it depends on the flow, and when to use pumps in series or in parallel.

A pump adds pressure to whatever reaches its inlet.
That pressure is measured as _lift_, in metres of head: the height of fluid column the pump could hold up.
How much a pump lifts depends on how much flow passes through it.

A pipeline pump on a gas line works as a compressor instead; see xref:compressors.adoc[].

== The pump curve

A pump lifts the most when nothing flows.
As the flow rises, its lift falls, through its _design point_, down to no lift at all at its _runout_ flow.

[cols="1,1,1,1"]
|===
| Pump | No flow | Design point | Runout (no lift)

| Mk1
| 32.7 m
| 25 m at 300 m³/min
| about 577 m³/min

| Mk2
| 65.3 m
| 50 m at 600 m³/min
| about 1,153 m³/min
|===

So a pump is not a fixed "head lift".
The network settles at the flow where the pump's lift equals what the network needs at that flow:
the height to climb, the pressure the machines want at their inlets, and the friction along the way.

TIP: Run a pump near its design point.
Near runout it lifts almost nothing.

A pump draws power for the work it does, so a pump moving little uses little.

Pumps are generally better than vanilla's: a Mk1 lifts up to 32.7 m against vanilla's 22 m, and no pipe rating caps the flow they move.

== Before a pump will start

* A pump only starts once its inlet pipe is full (primed).
* A pump whose inlet pressure is too low slows itself down rather than cavitate.
A pump far above its source, or at the end of a long suction line, may lift less than its curve says.

== Starting and stopping

Switched on, a pump ramps from rest to full speed over 10 s, and its lift grows with it.
Switched off or unpowered, it coasts down on its own inertia, still lifting a little as it slows.
Against a shut line a Mk1 takes about 10 s, and a Mk2 about 8 s, to fall to 30% speed.
A pump at rest blocks the line in both directions.

So pressure downstream of a pump comes and goes over seconds, not at once.

=== A pump with its power cut can keep turning

If something else is still pushing fluid through an unpowered pump (a higher tank, a drop, another pump upstream), that flow turns the impeller like a turbine.
The pump slows only until the flow balances it, and holds that speed, drawing no power.
A pump holding at 37% speed is passing a little over its rated flow.

An unpowered pump never stops the flow.
To stop a line, use a valve: shut it, or set its flow limit to 0.

== Negative lift

If something else drives more flow through a pump than its runout, the pump stops adding pressure and becomes a restriction.
That something might be another pump upstream, or a drop from a higher source.
Its panel then shows a *negative lift*, and it draws no power.

NOTE: The pump isn't broken.
It's in the wrong place, or too small.
Replace it with a Mk2, or remove it if the line doesn't need it.

.Example
====
A Mk1 on a Dissolved Silica line read 602 m³/min, a lift of −3.5 m, and 0 kW: just past its runout of about 577 m³/min.
Two Mk1s in parallel, or a Mk2, fixed it.
====

== Series or parallel

[horizontal]
In series:: pumps add pressure.
In parallel:: pumps add flow.

Two pumps in line each still carry the whole flow.
Near a pump's limit, two in series give little more than one.

Two pumps side by side split the flow, so each runs nearer its design point and lifts far more.
Two Mk1s in parallel give about 25 m at 600 m³/min.

TIP: Size each pump for the flow through it, not for the lift alone.

Build parallel branches alike (same length, bends and height) so they share the flow evenly.
Each pump has a check valve, so a weaker one isn't run backwards.

.Example: six Water Extractors feeding twelve Coal Generators
====
The generators need 540 m³/min and 18 m of lift.

Two Mk1s in series on the riser gave about 9 m, and the generators farthest along starved.
The same two Mk1s in parallel fixed it.
A single Mk2 (50 m at 600 m³/min) would also have worked.
====
