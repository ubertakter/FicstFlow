= Fluid mechanics primer
:description: The handful of ideas every other page relies on: pressure, head, friction, pump curves, and why gas is different.

You don't need an engineering degree to build with FicstFluids, but you do need a few words.
This page explains them once, so the rest of the manual can use them freely.

== Pressure drives flow

Fluid flows from where the pressure is high to where it is low.
Nothing else moves it.
A pump raises the pressure on its outlet side; a machine's inlet pulls fluid in only if the pressure there is high enough; a tank pushes with the weight of what it holds.

Every part of a network shares the same pressures, so a change anywhere is felt everywhere.
Add a machine, and the pressure at every other machine drops a little.

== Head: pressure measured in metres

For liquids, FicstFluids measures pressure as *head*, in metres.
Head is the height of a column of the liquid that the pressure could hold up.
A pump that adds 25 m of head could push water 25 m straight up, and no further.

Head makes heights easy to reason about:

* Climbing costs head metre for metre.
Pumping water up a 15 m cliff uses 15 m of head before anything else.
* Going down gives it back.
A pipe that drops 10 m gains 10 m of head at the bottom.

So you can add up a line's needs in metres: the height to climb, the head the machines want at their inlets, and the friction on the way.

== Gauge and absolute pressure

*Gauge* pressure is measured relative to the atmosphere around you: 0 gauge means atmospheric.
*Absolute* pressure counts from a perfect vacuum, so atmospheric is about 101 kPa absolute.

FicstFluids shows gauge pressure unless it says otherwise.
A negative gauge pressure means below atmospheric: suction.
An empty gas platform reads about −101 kPa gauge, which is vacuum.

Gas pressures are shown in kPa and MPa (1 MPa is 1,000 kPa, about ten times atmospheric).
Some gas figures in this manual also give psig, pounds per square inch gauge, because machine pressures were first set in it.

== Friction

Moving fluid rubs against the pipe wall, and that costs pressure along the pipe.
The loss grows:

* with the *length* of the pipe,
* steeply with the *flow*: twice the flow costs roughly four times the pressure,
* with *bends and fittings*,
* with a *narrow pipe*: a Mk2 pipe (1.5 m bore) loses far less than a Mk1 (1.25 m) at the same flow,
* with a *thick liquid*: Heavy Oil Residue is thousands of times more viscous than water in the model.

Friction is why FicstFluids pipes have no flow rating.
A pipe will carry as much as the pressure across it can push.
A short pipe can carry a great deal; a long one, much less.

== A pipe holds no pressure until it is full

A liquid line has to fill before it can push.
While pipes and machine buffers are filling, the water goes into them, not into pressure.
That is why starting a network takes some care (see xref:starting-up.adoc[]).

== Pump curves

A pump does not add a fixed head.
It lifts the most when nothing flows, and less the more you draw through it, down to nothing at all at its *runout* flow.
Its *design point* is the flow it was made for, and the one where it runs best.

The network settles where the pump's lift equals what the network needs at that flow.
Draw more, and the pump lifts less; the pressures drop until supply and demand match again.
The numbers for each pump are in xref:pumps.adoc[].

== Suction and cavitation

A pump can pull its inlet below atmospheric, but only so far.
Below a liquid's vapour pressure the liquid boils in the pipe, which is called *cavitation*.
FicstFluids' pumps protect themselves: a pump whose inlet pressure gets too low slows down rather than cavitate.

So a pump far above its source, or at the end of a long suction line, lifts less than its curve says.
Put pumps low, near their source, and feed them from above where you can.

== Gas is different

A liquid hardly compresses.
Push on one end of a full pipe and the other end moves at once.

A gas compresses.
Pushing gas into a pipe raises its pressure only gradually, as more gas packs in, so:

* a gas line *stores* gas as pressure, and holds far more than its volume at atmospheric would suggest,
* a long gas line takes a long time to come up to pressure,
* a line delivers less than it takes in while its pressure is rising, because the rest is packing into the pipe.

Gas flow is measured in *standard m³*: the volume the gas would have at atmospheric pressure.
That is why a gas wagon can hold far more "m³" than its tank's size.

Gas machines need far more pressure than liquid ones, measured in MPa rather than metres, and the only way to get there is a compressor.
